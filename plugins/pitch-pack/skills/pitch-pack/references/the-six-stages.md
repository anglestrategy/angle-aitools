# The six stages, expanded

A longer read on each of the six stages. Use this as the working sequence when running a project end to end.

## 1. Read what is in front of you

The single biggest failure mode in pitch work is paraphrasing the brief back to the client. Pull every fact instead.

What to extract:

- Project name, location, scale (sqm, km, capacity, opening date).
- Stated objectives (always written in client language — keep them verbatim).
- Audience (named groups and assumed groups).
- Constraints (budget, timing, regulatory, brand-system).
- Adjacent products or assets in the same client portfolio.
- Whoever else is working on this on the client side.

What to notice:

- Things named but never defined. (Waha example: "Experience Center" appeared in one objective but was undefined anywhere in the masterplan.)
- Things implied but never asked for.
- Tonal hints (does the brief read corporate, civic, cultural?).

Write a short "What's in the brief" memo. One page.

## 2. Name what is actually being solved

The brief says "launch the park." The strategic problem is usually different — it might be "make 3% of a project carry the weight of the whole story," or "build the platform that the rest of the masterplan will inherit," or "earn the right to be talked about at all."

Sharpen it down to one sentence. That sentence is what the rest of the work has to answer.

Test the sentence by writing two or three alternative diagnoses and asking which one would change the creative work most.

## 3. Map the audience

For most launches you will find two to four concentric audience rings:

- A **core** ring (small, high-stakes — investors, partners, media).
- A **primary** ring (large, defines whether the project becomes a habit — residents, families, neighbours).
- A **secondary** ring (spreads social proof — event-goers, tourists, content creators).

For each ring write:

- Who they are (one sentence)
- When they show up (day, night, weekend, event)
- How they read the project (what signal they take from it)
- What they need to feel for the project to land

Resist building four separate campaigns. The brief is to find one platform that bends to each ring.

## 4. Study comparable launches

Pick five to eight reference projects. For destinations, peer parks. For products, peer launches. For cultural projects, peer institutions.

For each one, write two tight reads:

- **How they positioned it.** One short paragraph.
- **What we take from it.** One short paragraph.

Do not write detailed case studies. The job is to lift the communication move worth borrowing, not to summarise the project.

End the section with a single pattern observation that connects the references.

## 5. Write what the work has to do

This is the strategic frame the creative team will build on. Five to seven demands, each a single sentence with a short paragraph underneath.

A good demand is:

- Strategic enough to guide creative choices.
- Specific enough that you can tell whether a piece of work is meeting it or not.
- Portable across phases of the project, not locked to the launch moment.

Example demands (from the Waha project):

- It has to scale across phases, audiences, and time.
- It has to read as a destination, not a development.
- It has to make sustainability feel like atmosphere.
- It has to set up what comes next.

## 6. Pass it to the creative team

Two pieces of output:

- **Things to avoid.** A short list of moves the platform should not make. The point is to be clear about it now, rather than discover it in revisions.
- **Three filters.** Three questions to read every piece of subsequent work through. Does it scale? Is it the right place? Does it set up what comes next? (These three are good defaults. Adjust the third one to the project.)

The creative work picks up from here. The strategy section ends.
